Wick selection is arguably the most consequential decision you'll make when developing a candle. Get it right and you'll have a clean, even burn, minimal soot, and a fragrance throw that fills a room. Get it wrong and you're dealing with tunnelling, mushrooming, excessive black smoke, or a flame that drowns in its own melt pool. This guide walks you through the core principles of wick sizing so you can build a reliable testing process from day one.

## Why Wick Sizing Is Not One-Size-Fits-All

The wick's job is to draw melted wax up through capillary action and deliver it to the flame at the right rate. Too little fuel and the flame will be weak, the wick will drown, and the candle will tunnel. Too much fuel and the flame will run hot, produce soot, and potentially create a safety hazard.

The "right rate" depends on several interconnected variables:

- **Container internal diameter** — the wider the vessel, the more wax needs to melt per hour to achieve a full melt pool
- **Wax type** — soy, coconut, paraffin, and blends all have different viscosities and melt points, which affect how quickly liquid wax travels up the wick
- **Fragrance and dye load** — higher fragrance oil concentrations and certain dyes can suppress or amplify wick performance
- **Wick construction** — flat-braided, square-braided, cored (cotton-paper, zinc, or wood), and ribbon wicks each behave differently

Because all these variables interact, no published sizing chart can replace physical burn testing in your specific setup.

## Understanding Wick Series and Their Characteristics

Wick manufacturers categorise wicks into series, and each series is designed to excel in particular wax types or container styles. Here are the most commonly encountered families:

### Cotton and Paper-Core Wicks (CD, CDN, ECO)

These are among the most versatile wicks on the market and are frequently recommended for container candles. The CD series uses a paper core that keeps the wick upright during the pour and tends to perform well in softer waxes such as soy and coconut blends. The ECO series has a natural cotton construction with a slight bend that can reduce carbon buildup on the tip. Both series are available in numbered sizes — higher numbers indicate a larger diameter and therefore a higher fuel-delivery rate.

### Square-Braided Cotton Wicks

Square wicks are traditionally associated with beeswax and paraffin. Their construction means they curl back on themselves as they burn, which helps the tip stay in the flame zone for consistent self-trimming. Suppliers typically recommend these for pillar candles and container candles with higher melt-point waxes.

### Wooden Wicks

Wooden wicks create a distinctive crackle sound and a wide, low flame that spreads heat laterally rather than concentrating it centrally. They perform particularly well in coconut and soy-coconut blends, which have a lower viscosity at burn temperature. Single-ply, double-ply, and booster configurations each suit different container diameters. Industry guidance recommends testing the next ply or width up if you notice the flame self-extinguishing.

### Pre-Tabbed vs. Bare Wicks

Most container candle makers will use pre-tabbed wicks — wicks already attached to a metal sustainer tab at the base. Bare wicks are available for pillar and moulded candles where you thread the wick yourself.

## Matching Wick Size to Container Diameter

Container internal diameter is the starting point for every wick selection. Most wick manufacturers publish a suggested diameter range for each wick in their series. Use these as a starting grid, not a final answer.

A general approach that experienced makers use:

1. **Measure the internal diameter** of your container at the widest point of the melt pool (usually at or near the wax surface, not the rim).
2. **Identify two or three candidate wick sizes** from the manufacturer's chart for that diameter range.
3. **Start with the middle candidate** for your first test burn.
4. **Adjust up or down** based on burn test results.

For containers under approximately 6 cm in diameter, a single wick is standard. For wider vessels — generally above 8–9 cm — you may need to test double-wicking. Two smaller wicks placed symmetrically often outperform one oversized wick because they create a more even melt pool and reduce the risk of an excessively large flame.

## Conducting a Proper Burn Test

A burn test is not a single afternoon's work. A methodical multi-session approach gives you reliable data.

**Session 1 — First two hours:**
Light the candle and allow it to burn until the melt pool reaches the edges of the container, or for two hours, whichever comes first. Observe and note:
- Flame height (ideally 1.5–2.5 cm for most container sizes)
- Whether the wick mushrooms (carbon buildup on tip)
- Any black soot on the jar walls
- Melt pool depth and whether it has reached full diameter

**Between sessions:**
Extinguish the candle. Allow it to fully resolidify (at least four hours). Trim the wick to approximately 5 mm. This simulates real consumer behaviour and ensures your tests reflect realistic use.

**Sessions 2–4:**
Repeat the burn, each time noting whether performance is consistent. A well-wicked candle should achieve a full melt pool within the first two hours of each burn session without producing excessive soot or an oversized flame.

**Keeping records:**
Create a simple burn log with columns for: wick code, wax type, fragrance percentage, container size, session number, flame height, melt pool diameter, observations, and pass/fail verdict. This log becomes invaluable when you need to reproduce a successful candle or troubleshoot a new one.

## Common Wick Problems and Adjustments

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Tunnelling (no full melt pool) | Wick too small | Try the next size up in the same series |
| Excessive soot / black smoke | Wick too large, or fragrance too high | Size down or reduce fragrance load |
| Flame drowning / self-extinguishing | Wick too small for wax viscosity | Try a higher-fuel series, or reduce fragrance load |
| Excessive mushrooming | Wax-fragrance combination deposits carbon rapidly | Try a series with a natural curl (ECO, square); reduce fragrance load |
| Flame too tall (over 3 cm) | Wick oversized | Size down |
| Uneven melt pool | Single wick insufficient for wide container | Trial double-wicking |

## Wick Sizing Checklist

Before signing off on a wick for production, work through this checklist:

- [ ] Burn test conducted for a minimum of four sessions (8+ total hours)
- [ ] Full melt pool achieved within 2 hours in every session
- [ ] Flame height consistently between 1.5 cm and 2.5 cm (or within supplier guidance for your container)
- [ ] No visible black soot on container walls after any session
- [ ] Wick does not drown or self-extinguish
- [ ] Mushrooming is minimal and clears quickly when trimmed
- [ ] Performance is consistent at varying ambient temperatures if relevant to your market
- [ ] Burn test repeated with the actual fragrance oil and dye load you plan to use in production

## Fragrance Load and Its Effect on Wick Performance

Fragrance oil is not inert in the wick system. Fragrance oils contain varying proportions of carrier (often a diluent such as DPG or a fractionated oil) and aromatic compounds. High fragrance loads — typically anything above the wax manufacturer's recommended maximum — can clog the wick's capillary channels, suppress the burn, or cause the flame to sputter. They can also lower the flash point of the wax-fragrance blend.

Suppliers typically recommend fragrance loads between 6% and 10% for most soy blends and up to 12% for some paraffin blends, though these figures are guidelines rather than absolute limits. Always consult your specific wax supplier's technical data sheet.

If you change your fragrance percentage, even slightly, re-test your wick. A formulation that burns beautifully at 8% fragrance load may underperform at 10%.

## Final Thoughts

Wick sizing is a process, not a lookup table. Every combination of container, wax, fragrance, and dye is its own micro-ecosystem. The makers who produce the most consistent candles are those who treat burn testing as a non-negotiable step rather than a shortcut to skip. Invest the time upfront, document everything, and you'll build a reliable library of proven formulations that makes future product development faster and more predictable.
