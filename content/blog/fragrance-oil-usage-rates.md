One of the most common questions new candle and wax melt makers ask is: "How much fragrance oil should I add?" It sounds like a simple question, but the answer requires understanding how fragrance behaves across different applications, what "maximum usage rate" actually means, and why testing is the only reliable way to land on the right number for your specific formulation.

## What Usage Rate Means — and What It Doesn't

Usage rate, sometimes called fragrance load or scent load, refers to the percentage of fragrance oil relative to the total weight of your batch. A 10% usage rate in a 500 g candle means 50 g of fragrance oil and 450 g of wax.

The maximum usage rate published by a fragrance supplier is the upper limit at which the fragrance has been tested to bind with a particular medium without causing performance issues. Exceeding this figure can lead to:

- Fragrance oil pooling or "sweating" on the surface of the finished product
- Seeping or leaking from the container
- Inconsistent burn performance in candles (drowning wicks, spitting)
- Skin sensitisation risks if the product is intended for body use

**Important distinction:** The maximum usage rate is not the recommended usage rate. Many fragrances perform beautifully at lower concentrations. Working at or near the maximum does not automatically produce a stronger scent throw; it may simply produce a formulation that is harder to work with.

## Usage Rates by Application

Different products have very different relationships with fragrance. What is safe and effective in one medium may be inappropriate in another.

### Container Candles

For most soy-based container waxes, suppliers typically recommend a fragrance load of between 6% and 10% by weight. Paraffin waxes tend to have a higher fragrance-binding capacity, with some blends accepting up to 12%. Coconut wax blends are prized partly because they can bind fragrance effectively at moderate loads while still delivering strong cold and hot throw.

Key considerations for candle makers:
- Higher loads do not linearly increase scent throw. Beyond a certain point, excess fragrance oil sits unbound in the wax and can cause surface issues or wick problems.
- Flash point matters. Fragrance oils with a flash point below approximately 65°C should be added to wax that has cooled to a temperature comfortably above the flash point but below the point where rapid volatilisation occurs. Suppliers typically recommend adding fragrance at around 65–70°C for most soy waxes, but consult your specific wax's technical data sheet.

### Wax Melts

Wax melts tolerate higher fragrance loads than container candles because there is no wick or combustion to worry about. Loads of 10–15% are common, and some high-absorption waxes designed specifically for melts can accept up to 18% or beyond according to industry guidance.

The higher the fragrance load in a wax melt, the more pronounced the cold throw tends to be — the scent you can smell before the melt is warmed. For retail display, cold throw can be a significant selling point.

Bear in mind that snap bars, clamshells, and other melt formats are handled by customers, so any fragrance oil that seeps out of the wax creates a mess and a potential allergen exposure issue.

### Reed Diffusers

Reed diffusers operate on a completely different principle. The fragrance is typically diluted in a carrier solvent — commonly dipropylene glycol (DPG), isopropyl myristate, or a fractionated carrier — rather than bound in wax. Ratios commonly suggested in the industry range from 20% to 30% fragrance oil in the total diffuser volume, though this varies considerably based on the intensity of the fragrance oil itself.

Viscosity matters in diffusers. If the base solvent is too thin, the reeds will draw up liquid too quickly and exhaust the fragrance rapidly. If it is too thick, the reeds will barely diffuse anything. Some makers blend DPG with a small proportion of water or alcohol to adjust viscosity.

### Room Sprays and Linen Mists

These typically use a high proportion of alcohol (usually 90%+ isopropyl or denatured) as the carrier, with fragrance loads often sitting between 10% and 20%. The alcohol acts both as a solvent and as a propellant for the scent molecules.

## Why Getting the Usage Rate Wrong Creates Problems

### Too Little Fragrance

Under-fragrancing a candle is the easier mistake to correct, but it's still a mistake. The product will have weak cold throw and disappointing hot throw. In wax melts, a low fragrance load often means the melt smells pleasant for the first warming session but has little left to give afterward.

### Too Much Fragrance

Over-fragrancing has more serious consequences:

**In candles:** Excess fragrance oil that cannot bind to the wax separates and pools. This can accumulate around the wick, potentially creating unpredictable flare-ups. In extreme cases, it can contribute to a fire hazard.

**In wax melts:** Seeping or wet surfaces make the product look poorly made and can leave oily residue on warmers and surfaces.

**In body products:** Any product applied to skin must comply with the fragrance supplier's recommended skin-safe usage rates, which are almost always much lower than those for candles. Applying a candle-formulated fragrance load directly to skin is not appropriate and may cause sensitisation or irritation.

## Building a Systematic Testing Process

There is no substitute for physical testing, but you can make the testing process efficient with a structured approach.

### Step 1 — Establish a Baseline

Start at the midpoint of the supplier's recommended range. If the recommended range is 6–10%, begin at 8%. This gives you room to move in either direction based on your results.

### Step 2 — Conduct Cold and Hot Throw Tests

Cold throw: After the candle has fully cured (suppliers typically recommend a cure time of at least 48 hours for soy, and up to two weeks for optimal performance), smell the unlit candle at arm's length. Rate the scent intensity on a consistent scale (e.g., 1–5).

Hot throw: Burn the candle for two hours in a standardised room size. Note how quickly the scent fills the space and how long it persists.

### Step 3 — Iterate in Increments

Adjust by 1–2% at a time, not in large jumps. Reformulating from 8% to 12% in one go skips potentially optimal points and wastes materials.

### Step 4 — Stability Testing

Once you have identified your working fragrance load, let finished products sit for two to four weeks and re-examine for:
- Surface seeping or sweating
- Colour change or discolouration
- Any scent fade from the original

### Step 5 — Document Everything

Record batch number, wax type, fragrance oil name and lot number, pour temperature, fragrance addition temperature, and your test results. This documentation becomes essential if you need to troubleshoot a future batch or scale up production.

## Practical Fragrance Usage Rate Checklist

Use this before finalising any new formulation:

- [ ] Checked fragrance supplier's maximum recommended usage rate for your application
- [ ] Started testing at the midpoint of the recommended range
- [ ] Allowed adequate cure time before conducting throw tests
- [ ] Conducted both cold and hot throw assessments (for candles and melts)
- [ ] Checked for surface seeping or sweating at 1, 2, and 4 weeks
- [ ] Verified no negative impact on wick performance (for candles)
- [ ] Confirmed fragrance load complies with skin-safe limits if product contacts skin
- [ ] All test results documented with batch details

## A Note on Fragrance Concentration vs. Scent Throw

Fragrance oil concentration and perceived scent throw are related but not identical. Some fragrance oils are formulated at high concentration by the manufacturer — meaning a smaller quantity delivers a stronger scent. Others are more dilute and need a higher addition rate to achieve the same impact.

This is why a fragrance that smells overwhelming in the bottle may disappoint at 10% in a candle, while a more subtle fragrance at 8% may fill a room. The fragrance supplier's technical data sheet and, where available, their recommended usage rates specific to your application, are your best starting point. Beyond that, testing is the only honest answer.
