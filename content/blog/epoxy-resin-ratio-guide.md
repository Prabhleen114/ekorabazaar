# Epoxy Resin to Hardener Ratios Explained for Makers

Epoxy resin casting has become a cornerstone craft for Indian makers creating river tables, ocean wave art, jewelry, coaster sets, and preservation keepsakes. Yet resin is unforgiving: unlike clay or wax, which solidify through physical cooling or drying, epoxy hardens through a strict stoichiometric polymerization reaction between epoxide monomers (Part A) and polyamine hardeners (Part B).

If your ratio is off by even 3% to 5%, your cast may remain perpetually sticky, bendable, cloudy, or prone to extreme yellowing. In high-volume production or commercial orders, an unhardened pour means wasted silicone moulds, lost pigments, and discarded raw materials costing thousands of rupees.

This comprehensive guide breaks down the chemical mechanics of epoxy resin ratios, how to calculate mixtures by weight versus volume, how Indian ambient temperatures affect curing, and practical troubleshooting steps to guarantee hard, glass-like casts every time.

## 1. The Chemistry of Stoichiometric Cross-Linking

Epoxy resin consists of two distinct components formulated to react in precise molecular balance:
- **Part A (Resin):** Typically a diglycidyl ether of bisphenol-A (DGEBA) or bisphenol-F, containing reactive three-membered epoxide rings.
- **Part B (Hardener):** An aliphatic, cycloaliphatic, or aromatic polyamine containing active amine hydrogen atoms.

When mixed, each amine hydrogen opens an epoxide ring, forming a covalent carbon-nitrogen bond and a hydroxyl group. This cross-linking process proceeds in three dimensions, transforming liquid monomers into a rigid thermoset polymer network.

### Why "Adding More Hardener" Fails
A common misconception among beginner resin artists is assuming that adding extra hardener will accelerate curing on cold winter days. In epoxy chemistry, this causes immediate failure:
- **Excess Hardener (Part B):** Unreacted amine molecules remain trapped within the matrix, acting as internal plasticizers. The finished piece remains rubbery, greasy to the touch, and exhibits severe amine blush (a cloudy, sticky wax-like film).
- **Excess Resin (Part A):** Epoxide rings left without amine partners cannot cross-link. The resin stays tacky, soft, and mechanically weak.

The only way to accelerate an epoxy cure safely is through thermal management (ambient warmth), never by altering the manufacturer's specified stoichiometric ratio.

## 2. Common Commercial Ratios: 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, and 100:44

Different casting applications demand different resin formulations, which dictate their specific mixing ratios:

### 1:1 Ratio (By Volume) — Art & Coating Resins
- **Viscosity:** Medium to high (2,000 to 4,000 mPa.s).
- **Pot Life:** Short (25 to 45 minutes at 25°C).
- **Maximum Pour Depth:** 3 mm to 10 mm per layer.
- **Best Applications:** Canvas coatings, fluid art, geode coasters, sealing wood slabs, surface doming.
- **Why It Exists:** Formulated for consumer convenience; easy to measure equal volumes without digital scales.

### 2:1 Ratio (By Weight or Volume) — General Casting & Jewelry
- **Viscosity:** Low to medium (800 to 1,500 mPa.s).
- **Pot Life:** Moderate (45 to 90 minutes).
- **Maximum Pour Depth:** 15 mm to 30 mm.
- **Best Applications:** Silicone mould casting, bookmarks, pyramid paperweights, floral preservation, multi-layer trays using moulds from our [precision studio moulds collection](/wholesale/precision-studio-moulds).

### 3:1 Ratio (By Weight) — Deep Cast & River Table Systems
- **Viscosity:** Water-thin (200 to 500 mPa.s).
- **Pot Life:** Extended (3 to 6 hours).
- **Cure Time:** 48 to 72 hours at 25°C.
- **Maximum Pour Depth:** 50 mm to 100 mm in a single pour.
- **Best Applications:** Thick live-edge river tables, large sphere castings, botanical blocks.
- **Why It Exists:** Slow-reacting amines release exothermic heat gradually, preventing the resin from boiling, yellowing, or cracking during deep pours.

## 3. Weight vs. Volume: The Density Difference Trap

One of the costliest errors in resin crafting is confusing **ratio by weight** with **ratio by volume**.

Resin (Part A) and hardener (Part B) have different specific gravities (densities):
- Part A typically has a density of approximately 1.15 to 1.18 g/ml.
- Part B typically has a density of approximately 0.95 to 1.02 g/ml.

Because Part A is denser than Part B, a 100 ml cup of resin weighs roughly 116 grams, whereas a 100 ml cup of hardener weighs only 98 grams.

If your resin specifies a **2:1 ratio by weight**:
- 100 grams of Part A requires exactly 50 grams of Part B.
- If you mistakenly measure 100 ml of Part A and 50 ml of Part B, you have added 116 g of resin to 49 g of hardener—an unbalance that can compromise structural hardness.

Always check your product's technical data sheet (TDS). In a professional studio, **measuring by weight using a digital scale calibrated to 0.1 grams is significantly more reliable** than visual meniscus readings in plastic graduated cups.

## 4. Temperature and Exothermic Heat in Indian Climates

India's climate introduces unique seasonal challenges for resin artisans:

### Summer Considerations (35°C to 45°C ambient)
Epoxy reaction rates double with approximately every 10°C increase in temperature. In peak summer across North and Central India, working in a non-air-conditioned studio drastically shrinks your pot life:
- A 1:1 art resin with a 45-minute working time at 25°C may begin flash-curing within 15 to 20 minutes in a 38°C room.
- Pouring deep layers in summer can trigger a thermal runaway exotherm: the resin turns smoking hot, turns amber-yellow, bubbles violently, and can melt your [silicone moulds](/wholesale/general-silicone-moulds).
- *Solution:* Mix smaller batches (under 300 grams at a time), pour in shallower layers, and work in air-conditioned or early-morning environments.

### Monsoon and Humidity Considerations (RH > 70%)
During monsoon season across coastal hubs like Mumbai, Chennai, and Kolkata, high atmospheric humidity reacts with unreacted amines on the curing surface, causing **amine blush**:
- The surface develops a greasy, matte, or sticky haze.
- Moisture can also react with Part A to create micro-foaming (cloudy micro-bubbles that will not pop).
- *Solution:* Run a dehumidifier or air conditioner to keep relative humidity below 60% during the first 12 hours of cure.

### Winter Considerations (10°C to 18°C ambient)
In North Indian winters (Delhi-NCR, Punjab, UP), resin thickens like cold honey, trapping air bubbles and failing to cure fully within 24 hours.
- *Solution:* Place your sealed Part A and Part B bottles in a warm water bath (around 35°C) for 15 minutes before mixing to lower viscosity. Maintain room warmth with an oil heater during the cure cycle.

## 5. Step-by-Step Studio Mixing Protocol

Follow this standard operating procedure for consistent, crystal-clear casts:

1. **Calculate and Weigh Accurately:** Place a clean polypropylene mixing cup on a digital scale, tare to zero, pour Part A to the exact gram weight, tare to zero again, and pour Part B.
2. **First Stir (3 to 5 Minutes):** Scrape the bottom and sidewalls continuously with a flat wooden or silicone spatula. Mix slowly in steady figure-eights to avoid whipping air into the liquid. The mixture will turn cloudy and stripy before returning to optical clarity.
3. **The Two-Pot Method:** Transfer the entire mixture into a second clean container and mix for another 1 to 2 minutes with a clean spatula. This ensures that unmixed residue from the sidewalls of the first container does not end up on your project.
4. **Degassing:** Allow the mixed resin to rest for 3 to 5 minutes so trapped micro-bubbles rise to the surface. Pop surface bubbles with an isopropyl alcohol mist (99%) or gentle passes of a heat gun held 15 cm away.
5. **Add Colorants:** Blend in cosmetic-grade micas or liquid resin pigments such as our [30ml liquid color pigments](/products/5), keeping colorant additions below 5% of total resin weight to avoid plasticizing the cure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I mix resin from one brand with hardener from another brand?
No. Never mix components across brands or product lines. Even if both products are labelled "2:1 ratio", their specific molecular weights, amine values, and epoxide equivalent weights (EEW) differ, leading to unreacted chemical residues and soft casts.

### How do I fix a resin piece that cured slightly bendy or sticky?
If the surface has a greasy film (amine blush), wash it thoroughly with warm water, dish soap, and a scrub pad. Let it dry completely, sand lightly with 220-grit wet sandpaper, and pour a fresh, precisely measured topcoat of 1:1 art resin. If the core of the piece remains jelly-like, the ratio was fundamentally incorrect; the piece cannot be cured and must be discarded.

### What is the maximum safe single-pour depth for standard 2:1 resin?
Most standard 2:1 casting resins safely tolerate depths of 15 mm to 25 mm in open silicone moulds. For pours deeper than 30 mm, pour in multiple successive layers after the previous layer reaches a firm gel state (tacky to the touch but not liquid), or switch to a specialized 3:1 deep-cast system.

### Can I use epoxy resin in food-contact tableware?
While fully cured epoxy is chemically inert, general craft epoxy resins are not certified for direct hot food contact or microwave/dishwasher use. For charcuterie boards, keep the resin strictly on decorative handles or non-food sections, and treat wooden food-contact zones with pure mineral oil and beeswax.
